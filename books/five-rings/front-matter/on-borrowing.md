# On Borrowing

This book is taken from another one.

In 1645, at sixty, having killed at least sixty men in duels, Miyamoto Musashi climbed a mountain in
Kyushu and wrote *Go Rin No Sho* — *The Book of Five Rings*. He finished it a few weeks before he died.
It is a manual for killing people with a sword, and it is not written in metaphor. This book borrows its
structure, much of its thinking, and the shape of its sentences.

Books like this one usually go wrong in the same way, so it is worth saying in advance what was done
about it.

**There is no enemy here.** Musashi's entire frame is a person trying to kill you. Software has no such
person, and every attempt to supply one — the competitor, the other team, the reviewer, the rival
engineer — makes the work worse and the workplace worse. Where a passage of the original depends on an
opponent, this book supplies something that is not a person: the system's actual behaviour, which does
not care what you believe about it; entropy; complexity; and most often your own prior assumptions, which
are the real adversary in nearly every hard afternoon. Where a passage cannot be read without an
opponent — Musashi has sections on crushing a weaker man and on destroying his morale — it was dropped
rather than softened. Softening it would have kept the disposition and lost only the words.

**Nothing was kept for atmosphere.** A borrowed idea earns its place only if the engineering version is
useful with the quotation struck out. If removing Musashi leaves nothing but a mood, the section was
decoration, and decoration ranked above substance is the exact fault his first book is written to attack.

**The stakes are not inflated.** Musashi's stakes were mortal. Ours are not. There is no war here, no
battle, no shipping-is-life-or-death. The real pressures of this work — a system people depend on, data
that does not come back, someone's payroll, a night of sleep — are enough, and have the advantage of
being true.

**No tool is named.** Musashi deliberately refused to name the schools he criticised, on the grounds that
doctrine varies by branch and that naming turns the argument into a dispute about the naming. The failure
modes outlive every instance of them. A chapter attacking a particular framework would be partisan on the
day it was written and unreadable within five years.

**Every criticism carries its exception.** Mid-attack on schools that favour very long swords, Musashi
stops to say: do not unconditionally dislike long swords; the fault is dependence on them. That check is
copied here. A criticism without the conditions under which the criticised thing is right is a school
selling flowers.

**One passage was left untranslated.** The last book ends in something religious that does not convert
into engineering. Producing a plausible-sounding paraphrase of it would be the counterfeit that same book
opens by condemning. It is quoted at the end and left alone.

Finally, and this is Musashi's own instruction repeated three times in his second book: reading this
does nothing. He says *do not just read, memorise or imitate*, and closes with *teach your body
strategy*. Every section here ends in something to do, and the ones you do not do are the ones that were
not transferred.

---

*Quotations are from the Victor Harris English translation (1974). Musashi is quoted, credited, and
disagreed with by name where he deserves it. This is not a translation, an interpretation, or a
discovery of hidden meaning. It is a new book that took its structure from an old one and says so.*
