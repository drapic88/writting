# Adaptation Rules

Rules for turning Musashi's material into engineering material. They exist because this genre — "ancient
warrior wisdom for modern professionals" — has a well-known failure mode, and the failure is not a matter
of taste. It produces real harm in engineering organisations: manufactured urgency, colleagues framed as
adversaries, overwork valorised as discipline.

The rules are constraints on the derived book, not on the reading. The notes record everything, including
what is rejected.

---

## Rule 1 — There is no enemy, and one must not be invented

Musashi's frame is a person trying to kill you. Software engineering has no such person. Every attempt to
supply one — the competitor, the other team, the reviewer, the product manager, the "10x" rival — produces
a worse engineer and a worse workplace.

Where a passage depends on an opponent, the derived reading must supply something that is not a person:

- the system's actual behaviour, which does not care what you believe about it
- entropy: the drift of a codebase, the decay of a runbook, the rot of a dependency
- complexity, which grows unless spent against
- your own prior assumptions, which are the most frequent real adversary

Where a passage *cannot* be read without an opponent — "To Crush," "To Penetrate the Depths" — it is
dropped, visibly, with the reason stated. Softening such passages into acceptable language is worse than
dropping them, because the disposition survives the rewording.

## Rule 2 — Nothing is borrowed for atmosphere

A passage earns its place only if the engineering reading is *useful without the quotation*. If removing
Musashi leaves nothing but a mood, the passage was decoration — and decoration over substance is exactly
what the Ground Book's "nut and the flower" condemns. A book that fails this test is refuted by its own
source.

Practical test for every section of the derived book: strike the quotation and the Japanese term. Is
there still a claim an engineer could act on tomorrow? If not, cut the section.

## Rule 3 — No manufactured stakes

Musashi's stakes were real and mortal. Ours are not, and inflating them is dishonest. No death, no war, no
battle, no "shipping is life or death." The genuine pressures of the work — a system people depend on,
irreversible data loss, someone's payroll, a night of sleep — are enough, are true, and are more useful
because they are true.

Corollary: nothing in the derived book may valorise overwork. Musashi trained relentlessly, and any
chapter on practice must make the distinction between deliberate practice and grinding, because the
source does not make it for us.

## Rule 4 — Criticise patterns, never named tools

Directly from the Wind Book: Musashi deliberately did not name the schools he attacked, because doctrine
varies by branch and the naming becomes the argument. A chapter attacking a specific framework is
partisanship on publication and unreadable in five years. The failure mode outlives every instance of it.

## Rule 5 — Every criticism carries its exception

"Great and small go together." Musashi checks his own polemic mid-attack: do not unconditionally dislike
long swords; the fault is dependence on them. Every criticism in the derived book states the conditions
under which the criticised practice is right. A criticism without its exception is a school selling
flowers.

## Rule 6 — The book must state its own insufficiency

The Water Book says three times that reading it is not enough, and closes with "Teach your body strategy."
A derived book that presents itself as sufficient contradicts the tradition it claims. It must say plainly
that reading it changes nothing, and it must be structured so that each chapter ends in something to
*do*.

## Rule 7 — Keep hierarchy out

The Ground Book's four estates and its sorting of people into permanent grades of ability are a
description of Tokugawa society, not an insight. "Match work to demonstrated strength" transfers.
"Some people are floor joists" does not. People grow, rotate, and are not timber.

## Rule 8 — Leave something untranslated

The Void Book's final turn is religious and does not convert. Producing a plausible engineering
paraphrase of it would be the counterfeit that same book opens by condemning. Acknowledging one passage
as untranslatable is more credible than translating everything, and it signals that the rest of the
translations were earned.
